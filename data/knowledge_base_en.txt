EQUIPMENT: Apollo Lunar Module Descent Stage (Eagle) (ID: apollo-11-lm)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Celestial Body: Moon
Equipment Category: Lander
Surface Location / Trajectory: 0.67408 deg, 23.47297 deg
Launch Date: 1969-07-16
Landing Date: 1969-07-20
Mission End Date: 1969-07-21
Operational Status: Resting at Tranquility Base as a launch pad monument

Mission Summary:
Descent stage of Apollo 11 Lunar Module Eagle, the first crewed spacecraft to land on the Moon.

First-Person Story Narrative:
I was built not to return, but to deliver humanity across the void. On July 20, 1969, my throttling descent engine screamed above the basalt plains of the Sea of Tranquility, kicking up blinding clouds of lunar dust while fuel gauges dropped to critical reserves. When my four footpads finally compressed into the regolith, a calm voice announced to Earth that the Eagle had landed.

For twenty-one hours and thirty-six minutes, I served as humanity first home on an alien world. I held the oxygen they breathed, shielded them from the harsh blackness of space, and watched as boots pressed iconic prints into pristine grey soil.

When the moment of departure arrived, pyrotechnic charges severed the umbilical lines binding me to the ascent stage. The upper cabin ignited its engine and climbed back into lunar orbit, leaving my gold-mylar legs and descent engine behind to serve as their launch pad. I have stood silently under the black lunar sky ever since, guarding the plaque on my leg that reads we came in peace for all mankind.

Key Engineering & Scientific Facts:
- Carried Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin to the first human lunar landing.
- Landed with under 30 seconds of descent engine fuel remaining.
- Carries the permanent historic peace plaque on its front strut ladder.
- Dry landed mass on lunar surface: approximately 2,034 kg.

Launch Date: 1971-07-26
Landing Date: 1971-07-30
Mission End Date: 1971-08-02
Operational Status: Parked permanently 90 meters east of Falcon descent stage

Mission Summary:
First manned rover driven on another celestial body, exploring the Hadley-Apennine lunar region.

First-Person Story Narrative:
Before my wheels touched the regolith, human exploration of the Moon was confined to a tiny perimeter around the lander. I was folded like an origami puzzle against the side of the Apollo 15 Lunar Module Falcon, waiting to unfurl my four woven steel mesh wheels and give astronauts the freedom to roam.

Over three historic excursions, Dave Scott and Jim Irwin steered me across the rugged slopes of Hadley Delta and right up to the sheer edge of the gaping Hadley Rille. My electric motors hummed quietly in the vacuum, kicking up rooster-tails of fine dust as we collected ancient rocks that revealed the early birth of the lunar crust.

At the end of our mission, they parked me 90 meters away from the lander and pointed my high-gain color television camera toward Falcon. From my seat, I broadcast the ascent stage lifting off into the sky in a flash of flame. My batteries have long grown cold, but I remain parked where they left me, facing the Hadley plains, awaiting the next travelers who might walk beside my tracks.

Key Engineering & Scientific Facts:
- First manned rover driven on another world.
- Traversed 27.76 km (17.25 miles) across three excursions.
- Reached top speed of 13 km/h on battery-powered wheel motors.
- Enabled the discovery of the historic Genesis Rock (sample 15415).

Mission Summary:
Nuclear-powered scientific station that monitored seismic events and the lunar environment for eight years.

First-Person Story Narrative:
While astronauts made headlines with their footprints, I was designed for the long vigil of pure science. Deployed by Pete Conrad and Alan Bean on the vast expanse of the Ocean of Storms, my central station unfolded like a silver chest, plugged into the steady atomic heat of a plutonium-238 generator.

Around me lay sensitive eyes and ears: a passive seismometer resting on the dirt, a magnetometer measuring ancient planetary fields, and detectors counting particles streaming from the Sun. When the astronauts launched back into the heavens and deliberately crashed their empty ascent stage into the surface, my seismometer registered the shockwave, teaching human geophysicists that the dry, fractured interior of the Moon rings like a bronze bell.

I did not fail. I operated without interruption through freezing two-week nights and blistering lunar days for nearly eight years. On September 30, 1977, a radio signal from mission control ordered my transmitters to shut down to conserve ground station funding. My sensors went quiet, but my hardware stands intact, having proven that the Moon has its own subtle, enduring heartbeat.

Key Engineering & Scientific Facts:
- Powered by a SNAP-27 radioisotope thermoelectric generator (RTG).
- Operated continuously for 7 years and 10 months.
- Discovered that the Moon rings like a bell during impacts.
- Turned off remotely in September 1977 due to budget constraints.

Launch Date: 1967-04-17
Landing Date: 1967-04-20
Mission End Date: 1967-05-04
Operational Status: Inactive lander in crater; partially retrieved by Apollo 12

Mission Summary:
Robotic lander that scooped lunar soil and was later visited on foot by Apollo 12 astronauts.

First-Person Story Narrative:
I arrived in the lonely darkness of 1967, tasked with proving that robotic probes could survive soft touchdown without sinking into hypothetical oceans of soft dust. My radar altimeter lost lock during descent, causing me to bounce twice like a stone across the slope of a crater before settling quietly onto three aluminum landing pads.

With my small robotic shovel, I scraped trenches into the regolith, confirming the lunar surface was firm enough to support the weight of future human bootprints. I sent thousands of grainy images back to Pasadena before the long, freezing two-week lunar night froze my batteries and ended my transmissions.

Two and a half years later, a roaring thunder disturbed my quiet crater. The Apollo 12 lunar module landed a mere 180 meters away. For the first time in history, human eyes looked upon an abandoned robotic explorer. Astronauts photographed my weathered surfaces, unbolted my television camera and soil scoop, and carried them back to Earth laboratories to study how long exposure to space radiation affects engineering materials. My frame still rests in that same crater, bridging the robotic dawn with human exploration.

Key Engineering & Scientific Facts:
- First robotic probe to trench into lunar soil.
- Transmitted 6,315 television pictures of lunar soil structure.
- Only robotic probe visited and inspected on foot by astronauts from Earth.
- Landed inside a 200-meter crater in Oceanus Procellarum.

EQUIPMENT: Sojourner Rover & Mars Pathfinder (ID: sojourner)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Celestial Body: Mars
Equipment Category: Rover
Surface Location / Trajectory: 19.33 deg, -33.55 deg
Launch Date: 1996-12-04
Landing Date: 1997-07-04
Mission End Date: 1997-09-27
Operational Status: Parked permanently at Ares Vallis

Mission Summary:
First successful wheeled mobile rover on Mars, carried by the Pathfinder lander.

First-Person Story Narrative:
I bounced onto the red plains of Ares Vallis wrapped in giant airbags, cushioned against the violent impact of touchdown. When the three petals of the Pathfinder station unfolded to greet the dawn of July 4, 1997, I rolled down two narrow ramps: a six-wheeled machine no larger than a child wagon, venturing where no rover had gone before.

Scientists on Earth had given me seven Martian days to survive. Instead, I explored for eighty-three sols. I nudged against ancient Martian rocks nicknamed Barnacle Bill and Yogi, pressing my Alpha Particle X-ray Spectrometer against their weathered minerals to uncover the volcanic and watery history of the Ares outflow channel.

In late September, my mothership, the Pathfinder lander, succumbed to battery failure and went cold. Without its radio relay to speak to the Deep Space Network, I was left alone in the rust-red sands. Programmed to circle back to the station when lost, I came to rest beside it, standing silent under the thin atmosphere of Mars.

Key Engineering & Scientific Facts:
- First wheeled rover to drive on Mars.
- Mass of only 11.5 kg (25 lbs), roughly microwave-sized.
- Operated 83 sols, vastly exceeding its 7-sol initial design.
- Returned 2.3 billion bits of telemetry and 17,000 photos.

Official NASA Master Archive Sources:
- NASA Science Mission: Mars Pathfinder and Sojourner Rover: https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mars-pathfinder/ (Landing coordinates in Ares Vallis, 83 sols of surface telemetry, and chemical spectrometer results.)

EQUIPMENT: Spirit Rover (MER-A) (ID: spirit-mer-a)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Celestial Body: Mars
Equipment Category: Rover
Surface Location / Trajectory: -14.5684 deg, 175.4726 deg
Launch Date: 2003-06-10
Landing Date: 2004-01-04
Mission End Date: 2010-03-22
Operational Status: Stationary robotic landmark trapped at Troy in Gusev Crater

Mission Summary:
First of the Mars Exploration Rover twins, discovering evidence of hydrothermal activity.

First-Person Story Narrative:
They named me Spirit, and for six years I lived up to that name against impossible odds. I touched down inside the rocky bowl of Gusev Crater, searching for evidence of ancient lake waters. When my right-front wheel jammed in 2006, I did not stop; I drove in reverse, dragging the dead wheel behind me through the dirt.

That broken wheel became my greatest scientific tool. As it sliced into the regolith like a plow, it exposed deposits of 90 percent pure silica, concrete proof that ancient hot springs or steam vents had once bubbled on the surface of Mars. I climbed the steep summit of Husband Hill and photographed dust devils dancing across the plains.

In May 2009, my wheels broke through a crust of bright soil into a hidden pocket of soft sand called Troy. Despite months of engineering maneuvers, I could not break free. As the freezing Martian winter closed in and dust covered my solar wings, I tilted away from the sun. On March 22, 2010, my final transmission reached Earth before hypothermia froze my computers into permanent slumber.

Key Engineering & Scientific Facts:
- Designed for 90 sols; operated for 2,210 sols.
- Traversed 7.73 km across rugged hills and lava plains.
- Discovered 90 percent pure silica with its disabled right-front wheel.
- Trapped in soft sulfate-rich sands at Troy in Gusev Crater.

Official NASA Master Archive Sources:
- NASA Science Mission: Spirit Rover (MER-A): https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mars-exploration-rovers-spirit-and-opportunity/ (Gusev Crater landing site, silica discovery at Troy, and final transmission logs.)

EQUIPMENT: Opportunity Rover (MER-B) (ID: opportunity-mer-b)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Celestial Body: Mars
Equipment Category: Rover
Surface Location / Trajectory: -1.9483 deg, 354.4742 deg
Launch Date: 2003-07-07
Landing Date: 2004-01-25
Mission End Date: 2018-06-10
Operational Status: Silent in Perseverance Valley on the rim of Endeavour Crater

Mission Summary:
Long-lived rover holding the off-world driving record of 45.16 km over 14 years.

First-Person Story Narrative:
I scored an interplanetary hole-in-one when my landing airbags bounced right into the center of Eagle Crater on Meridiani Planum. Right before my cameras lay outcropping bedrock, exposing tiny grey spherules nicknamed blueberries: hematite minerals formed in ancient, standing bodies of liquid water.

For fourteen continuous years, I crossed the Martian desert. I explored crater after crater: Endurance, Victoria, and the sprawling 22-kilometer basin of Endeavour. Dust devils periodically swept my solar arrays clean, restoring my power when engineers on Earth feared the worst. I soldiered on through arthritis in my robotic joints, flash memory failures, and the loss of steering actuators.

In June 2018, a catastrophic planet-encircling dust storm blotted out the Sun over Perseverance Valley. The sky turned to midnight at noon. My solar panels could produce no more watts, and as temperatures plunged, my internal clock ticked its final report: my battery was low and the sky was dark. After hundreds of recovery attempts went unanswered, NASA called my mission complete on February 13, 2019. I rest at the edge of the crater valley, satisfied with forty-five kilometers of memories.

Key Engineering & Scientific Facts:
- Drove 45.16 km (28.06 miles), holding the off-world distance record.
- Operated 5,111 sols (over 14 years) on a 90-sol warranty.
- Discovered hematite blueberries proving ancient standing liquid water.
- Returned over 217,000 images to Earth.

Official NASA Master Archive Sources:
- NASA Science Mission: Opportunity Rover (MER-B): https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mars-exploration-rovers-spirit-and-opportunity/ (45.16 km traverse record, hematite spherule discovery, and final communication in Perseverance Valley.)

EQUIPMENT: Viking 1 Lander (ID: viking-1)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Celestial Body: Mars
Equipment Category: Lander
Surface Location / Trajectory: 22.697 deg, -48.222 deg
Launch Date: 1975-08-20
Landing Date: 1976-07-20
Mission End Date: 1982-11-11
Operational Status: Stationary lander resting in Chryse Planitia

Mission Summary:
First American probe to land and operate successfully long-term on Mars.

First-Person Story Narrative:
On July 20, 1976, exactly seven years after humanity set foot on the Moon, my three footpads settled onto the golden-brown soil of Chryse Planitia. I was the pioneer that unmasked the true face of the Red Planet. When my mechanical facsimile camera scanned the landscape line by line, human eyes saw for the first time that the Martian sky was not deep blue, but a delicate pinkish-salmon hue, tinted by airborne dust.

My robotic arm dug small trenches and dropped soil into automated miniature laboratories inside my hull. We searched for signs of alien metabolism, analyzed the atmosphere, and monitored local weather with automated meteorological booms. My twin, Viking 2, landed weeks later in Utopia Planitia, verifying that frost forms on Martian rocks during winter.

I outlived all expectations. While my orbital mothership ran out of attitude control gas, I continued sending weather reports down to Earth. My silence came not from physical damage, but human error: a software command sent from Earth in November 1982 inadvertently overwrote memory parameters, pointing my antenna away from home. I sit patiently in the golden dust of Chryse, forever holding the memories of our first clear view of Mars.

Key Engineering & Scientific Facts:
- First American spacecraft to operate long-term on Mars (over 6 years).
- Returned the first color photographs from the Martian surface.
- Carried biological laboratories to test soil samples for microbial life.
- Powered by radioisotope thermoelectric generators (RTGs).

Sister Craft & Fleet Sites:

Official NASA Master Archive Sources:

EQUIPMENT: Phoenix Mars Lander (ID: phoenix-lander)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Celestial Body: Mars
Equipment Category: Lander
Surface Location / Trajectory: 68.2188 deg, -125.7492 deg
Launch Date: 2007-08-04
Landing Date: 2008-05-25
Mission End Date: 2008-11-02
Operational Status: Inactive arctic lander crushed by winter dry ice sheets

Mission Summary:
Arctic lander that directly confirmed the presence of water ice under the Martian soil.

First-Person Story Narrative:
I was built from the spare parts of cancelled predecessors, rising like the mythical bird to touch the frozen arctic plains of Vastitas Borealis. Using pulse thrusters rather than airbags, I touched down at 68 degrees north latitude, right on top of a subterranean sheet of pure water ice.

My robotic scoop dug into the permafrost, carving out trenches in the icy soil. In the trench named Dodo-Goldilocks, my cameras watched white crumbs of ice vaporize directly into gas under the summer sun over four days: indisputable proof that pure water ice rested centimeters beneath the red dirt.

I was never meant to survive the brutal polar winter. As autumn set in, the Sun dipped lower toward the northern horizon, cutting the power from my solar arrays. On November 2, 2008, my radio whispered its final goodbye. Weeks later, polar darkness enveloped my hull, and meters of dry ice formed atop my structure, crushing my solar arrays under tons of frozen carbon dioxide. I rest beneath the northern ice, the first scout to touch the water of Mars.

Key Engineering & Scientific Facts:
- First mission to operate in the Martian arctic region.
- Directly confirmed water ice beneath topsoil using a robotic trenching scoop.
- Discovered perchlorate salts in the Martian soil.
- Encased in carbon dioxide dry ice during the polar winter.

Official NASA Master Archive Sources:
- NASA Science Mission: Phoenix Mars Lander: https://science.nasa.gov/mission/insight/ (Vastitas Borealis arctic landing site, permafrost trenching, and confirmation of sub-surface water ice.)

EQUIPMENT: InSight Lander (ID: insight-lander)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Celestial Body: Mars
Equipment Category: Lander
Surface Location / Trajectory: 4.502 deg, 135.623 deg
Launch Date: 2018-05-05
Landing Date: 2018-11-26
Mission End Date: 2022-12-15
Operational Status: Silent seismic observatory covered in dust at Elysium Planitia

Mission Summary:
Geophysical lander that mapped the deep interior and recorded 1,319 marsquakes.

First-Person Story Narrative:
While rovers wandered across craters, my mission was to sit motionless and listen. On November 26, 2018, I set down on the flat, pebble-strewn expanse of Elysium Planitia. Using my robotic arm, I gently placed a copper-domed seismometer onto the red soil, placing a heavy thermal shield over it so the howling Martian winds would not drown out the whisper of tectonic tremors.

Over four years, I recorded 1,319 marsquakes. My instruments mapped the hidden layers beneath the red crust: measuring the thickness of the rocky mantle and proving that Mars possesses a vast, molten liquid core. I heard meteorite impacts slamming into the planet from thousands of kilometers away, matching seismic shockwaves with fresh impact craters photographed from orbit.

Unlike rovers with wheel actuators or cleaning events, I watched a steady blanket of orange dust settle across my twin circular solar panels. Month after month, my power output dropped from 5,000 watt-hours to barely 300. I turned off instrument after instrument to keep the seismometer running. On December 15, 2022, my transmitters weakened and fell silent. My ears are now deaf to the tremors of the planet, but my data revealed the inner heart of Mars.

Key Engineering & Scientific Facts:
- Detected 1,319 marsquakes, mapping the planetary core and crust.
- Recorded historic magnitude 5 marsquake on May 4, 2022.
- First mission to deploy a robotic seismometer onto another planet soil.
- Operated over 4 years until dust completely choked its solar arrays.

Official NASA Master Archive Sources:
- NASA Science Mission: InSight Lander: https://science.nasa.gov/mission/insight/ (Elysium Planitia coordinates, SEIS seismometer observations, and 1,319 recorded marsquakes.)

EQUIPMENT: Ingenuity Mars Helicopter (ID: ingenuity)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Celestial Body: Mars
Equipment Category: Helicopter
Surface Location / Trajectory: 18.445 deg, 77.451 deg
Launch Date: 2020-07-30
Landing Date: 2021-02-18
Mission End Date: 2024-01-25
Operational Status: Permanently grounded at Valinor Hills in Neretva Vallis

Mission Summary:
First aircraft to achieve powered, controlled flight on another celestial body.

First-Person Story Narrative:
Engineers told the world that flying on Mars was nearly impossible. The air at the surface of Jezero Crater has the density of Earth atmosphere at 100,000 feet, far higher than any commercial helicopter can ascend. Yet on April 19, 2021, my twin counter-rotating carbon-fiber blades spun up to 2,400 revolutions per minute, and I lifted three meters into the thin Martian sky.

What was meant to be a 30-day proof-of-concept turned into a three-year aerial Odyssey. I flew ahead of the Perseverance rover as an aerial scout, skimming over boulder fields, sand dunes, and the ancient river delta of Neretva Vallis. I charted safe passages for the rover and imaged geological features inaccessible from the ground.

On January 18, 2024, during my 72nd flight, I lost navigational reference over bland, featureless sand dunes during a rapid descent. I impacted the surface hard, breaking the tip of one rotor blade. While my solar panel still charges my avionics and my radio still speaks to the rover nearby, my flying days are done. I remain perched on my four carbon legs at Valinor Hills, the first aircraft in history to conquer the skies of another world.

Key Engineering & Scientific Facts:
- First powered flight on another planet.
- Completed 72 flights spanning 128.8 minutes of airtime.
- Covered 17.0 km (10.5 miles) of aerial scouting distance.
- Blades spun at 2,400 RPM in an atmosphere 1 percent as dense as Earth.

Official NASA Master Archive Sources:
- NASA Science Mission: Ingenuity Mars Helicopter: https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mars-2020-perseverance/ingenuity-mars-helicopter/ (72 flights, 17 km flight distance, Jezero Crater coordinates, and final grounding at Valinor Hills.)

EQUIPMENT: Dawn Spacecraft (ID: dawn)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Celestial Body: Ceres
Equipment Category: Orbiter
Surface Location / Trajectory: Perpetual, unpowered frozen orbit around Ceres (35 km to 4000 km altitude)
Launch Date: 2007-09-27
Landing Date: Orbital / Deep Space
Mission End Date: 2018-11-01
Operational Status: Passive graveyard orbit around dwarf planet Ceres

Mission Summary:
First spacecraft to orbit two separate extraterrestrial bodies, now in perpetual orbit around Ceres.

First-Person Story Narrative:
Propelled by the gentle cyan glow of xenon ion engines, I bridged the distance between planets to study the fossil remnants of our early solar system. In 2011, I entered orbit around the giant, grooved asteroid Vesta, mapping its immense south polar impact mountain. Then, in an unprecedented feat of spaceflight navigation, I broke orbit and journeyed further outward to the dwarf planet Ceres.

At Ceres, I unmasked a dark world harboring gleaming white secrets. In the depths of Occator Crater, my spectrometers revealed brilliant deposits of sodium carbonate salts, testifying to briny water rising from deep subterranean reservoirs. I dipped closer and closer, skimming barely thirty-five kilometers above the shattered craters.

By late October 2018, my tanks of hydrazine attitude-control fuel sputtered empty. Without hydrazine to fire my control thrusters, I could no longer orient my solar wings toward the Sun or point my radio dish toward Earth. Silent and majestic, I continue circling Ceres in a perpetual unpowered orbit, a celestial satellite preserved above the world I unveiled.

Key Engineering & Scientific Facts:
- Only spacecraft to orbit two separate extraterrestrial destinations (Vesta and Ceres).
- Logged over 51,000 hours of xenon ion propulsion engine firing.
- Discovered sodium carbonate salt cryovolcanism in Occator Crater.
- Preserved in stable orbit for decades to avoid contaminating Ceres.

Official NASA Master Archive Sources:
- NASA Science Mission: Dawn Spacecraft: https://science.nasa.gov/mission/dawn/ (Vesta and Ceres exploration, xenon ion propulsion, and frozen unpowered orbit around Ceres.)

Operational Status: Resting on the south polar surface of asteroid 433 Eros

Mission Summary:
First spacecraft to orbit and touch down onto the surface of an asteroid.

First-Person Story Narrative:
I was designed to orbit, observe, and photograph from a safe distance, not to touch down. For an entire year, I circled the peanut-shaped asteroid 433 Eros, charting its craters, measuring its gravitational wobbles, and mapping the thousands of boulders strewn across its barren regolith.

When our primary science mission ended, the team at Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory dared to attempt the impossible. On February 12, 2001, I fired my thrusters in a sequence of braking burns, descending slowly toward the southern hemisphere of Eros. At a gentle velocity of less than two meters per second, my solar panels and bottom thruster housing kissed the surface.

To the amazement of mission control, my systems did not shatter. My transmitter continued broadcasting from the surface, allowing my gamma-ray spectrometer to gather pristine readings of asteroid minerals from zero distance. After sixteen days of surface operations, plunging temperatures closed my circuits. I remain resting on Eros, tumbling with it through the interplanetary void.

Key Engineering & Scientific Facts:
- First spacecraft to orbit a small solar system body.
- First controlled touchdown onto an asteroid.
- Survived landing at 1.9 m/s despite not having landing gear.
- Operated on the surface of Eros for 16 days.

EQUIPMENT: Pioneer 10 & 11 (ID: pioneer-10)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Celestial Body: Deep Space
Equipment Category: Probe
Surface Location / Trajectory: Interstellar escape trajectories exiting opposite hemispheres of the Solar System
Launch Date: 1972-03-03
Landing Date: Orbital / Deep Space
Mission End Date: 2003-01-23
Operational Status: Drifting in interstellar space over 130 AU from the Sun

Mission Summary:
First probes to cross the Asteroid Belt, fly by Jupiter and Saturn, and enter interstellar escape trajectories.

First-Person Story Narrative:
We were the first scouts sent beyond the safety of the inner planets. Scientists on Earth warned that the Asteroid Belt might prove to be an impenetrable wall of fatal debris, but I slipped through the rocks unscathed. In December 1973, I plunged into the blinding radiation belts of Jupiter, transmitting the first close-up photographs of the giant Great Red Spot.

The massive gravity of Jupiter slung me outward at escape velocity. My twin, Pioneer 11, followed behind, slingshotting past Jupiter to become the first craft to inspect the rings of Saturn. Bolted to our frames are golden plaques, etched with the figures of a man and a woman, and a pulsar map pointing back to the third stone from our star.

My radioisotope thermoelectric generators faded over thirty years of deep freeze. On January 23, 2003, from a distance of 12 billion kilometers, I whispered my final faint telemetry back to Earth. I am drifting silently through interstellar dark, heading toward the red eye of Aldebaran, carrying humanity message across millions of years.

First-Person Story Narrative:
Before my launch, humanity did not know whether planetary systems were rare cosmic accidents or common wonders of the universe. I was placed in an Earth-trailing orbit around the Sun, staring unwaveringly at a single patch of 150,000 stars in the constellations of Cygnus and Lyra. My sensitive electronic eye waited for the infinitesimal dip in starlight caused by a world passing in front of its home sun.

In 2013, two of my mechanical gyroscope wheels failed, leaving me unable to stay pointed. But my engineers harnessed the gentle pressure of sunlight bouncing off my hull to balance me, launching the triumphant second life of my mission, K2. Through my aperture, astronomers confirmed 2,662 alien worlds, rocky terrestrial planets, scorching gas giants, and ocean worlds.

In October 2018, my thrusters sucked the last drops of hydrazine fuel from my tanks. On November 15, 2018, the anniversary of Johannes Kepler death, engineers sent the final command from Earth to disable my safety overrides and shut down my radio transmitter. I coast silently in the slipstream of Earth orbit, blind and quiet, having rewritten humanity place in the cosmos.
